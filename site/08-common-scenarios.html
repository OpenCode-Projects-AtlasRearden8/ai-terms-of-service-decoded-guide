Eight Real-Life Scenarios, Decoded

The previous chapters explained the rules. This chapter applies them to the situations that actually come up in normal life - the moments where you are mid-type and suddenly think "...am I allowed to do this?"

Scenario 1: "I used AI to write a report for my job"

Typical terms let the app store and (if the training toggle allows) learn from the conversation, but they usually do not transfer your ownership of what you wrote. What that means in practice:

  • The company doesn't own your report. Most major consumer AI terms say you own your inputs and outputs.
  • The company might still see your report. Storage, review for safety, and (if the toggle is on) model training are usually all permitted.
  • Your employer's rules are a separate, stricter layer. Many workplaces forbid confidential data in consumer AI apps regardless of what the app's terms allow. When a workplace has an AI policy, the workplace policy wins - it applies to you even if the app would be fine with it.
  • Client-identifying details are the tripwire. Names of patients, customers, or students usually should not go in at all. Describe the shape of the problem, not the person's identity.

For real clinical or legal work, use whatever tool your employer's compliance team actually approved, and keep the consumer app for the useful-but-generic version of the task: education, drafting practice materials, translating a confusing form into plain English. That spirit is worth naming - AI is a good study partner and never the decision-maker about a person's care.

Scenario 2: "I'm writing a book with AI's help"

Honest, useful, mild-caveat breakdown:

  • Your manuscript is yours; the terms do not claim it.
  • The risk is not ownership. It is that your unpublished, pre-release work sits in a system whose data practices include retention (usually 30-90 days post-deletion) and - if you never turned the toggle off - model training.
  • If that thought bothers you for an unpublished project, do the same two clicks you did in chapter 2: turn the training toggle off, and prefer temporary/incognito chats for the actual manuscript pages. Sensitive-but-not-secret stuff (brainstorms, blurbs) can go anywhere.
  • For legal comfort about publishing rules (copyrightability of AI-assisted work, disclosure requirements on platforms), those are separate questions with changing answers - this guide does not try to answer them.

Scenario 3: "I shared a chat with a friend using a share link"

You made a "share" link, sent it to three friends, and now one of them passed it on to a group chat. What happens:

  • The link is not the same as ownership. Your friend reading it is fine; the conversation still lives in the app, still subject to the app's retention and any training settings that were active when it was created.
  • Shared-link conversations can sometimes be surfaced by search engines if the sharing feature allows public indexing - many apps have been tightening this after people accidentally made private conversations public. Assume anything shared by link could end up somewhere you didn't plan.
  • If a shared chat feels uncomfortable later, the right move is usually to delete the conversation from your history (which usually also kills the shared link), then double-check that it's actually gone by reopening the link.

Scenario 4: "My kid used my account"

Chapter 4 covered this fully; here is the trim version: the age clause is probably violated, the "one account per person" clause probably also is, and the fix that preserves future rights is simple - set the account up properly (youth/education versions, family or supervised plans where available, or a 13+ account your teen signs up for themselves with you in the room), and have the "don't tell the robot everything" conversation once, out loud.

Scenario 5: "The terms changed and I never got a real notification"

This one is common. When the terms say "we may update these terms, and your continued use means you accept," the practical playbook is:

  1. Notice often lands in a banner or an email you glanced past. Search your inbox for the app's name plus "terms" once a year.
  2. The five-minute checklist in chapter 7 is your friend in exactly this moment. Run it against the new version whenever there's an update worth noticing.
  3. If the changes are materially worse for you, your exit is the leverage. Export (chapter 5), close, and move. It is not dramatic; it is data hygiene.

Scenario 6: "I want my data gone - all of it - for good"

Delete the account through the app's own account-deletion path (not just "sign out"). Then:

  • Most apps keep data for their stated soft-delete window (often 30-90 days) before full deletion, minus whatever the terms carve out for legal or safety holds. This is normal and not something you can argue away; it is a fact about how software safety nets work.
  • Export first. Deleting first and exporting second never works, just as shredding a document and then copying it never works.
  • If an old conversation matters to you, save your own copy in your own files today. The "I'll clean it later" pile of modern life is now a pile of server-side generations, and "later" has a habit of not arriving.

Scenario 7: "Someone asked me to build an app on top of a free AI app"

The terms often include a section called "Acceptable Use," "Restrictions," or "Prohibited Uses," and it usually says you cannot resell the service or build a competing product on the free tier. If you are thinking of shipping something that routes other users' prompts through your personal free account, stop and check that clause. The intended tool for developers is usually a paid API with its own (very different) terms. There is an existing plain-English guide for exactly that - see the companion guides list at the bottom of this chapter.

Scenario 8: "Someone at the company asked for 'a copy of everything'"

Maybe you got an email or a form request. Whatever the reason, three principles help:

  • Verify who's asking. Real data access requests from an AI company come through the company's own verified channels, and are typically fulfilled via that account's settings, not by you emailing a file to a stranger.
  • You have a right to a copy of your own data under many privacy laws. Use the in-app export. If you cannot find the export, most major apps have a privacy-request form - search the app's help center for "request my data."
  • Do not send personal data through random channels. If someone asks you for a copy of someone else's data - a manager asking for an employee's chats, a stranger asking about your neighbors' accounts - the answer is almost always "they should open their own export from their own account." That is both a healthy boundary and usually the correct legal answer.

Quick reality check

Friend note: None of these scenarios require perfection. Every single one is survivable, and most are fixed by the same two moves: check the six lines of the five-minute checklist when a doubt shows up, and keep your own copy of anything you would hate to lose. That is the whole playbook. You've got this.
